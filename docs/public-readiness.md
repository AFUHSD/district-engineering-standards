# Getting a repository ready to go public

Peninsula publishes most of what it builds under MIT. An operational repository commonly names the systems it
runs on, so it should not be published directly. The goal is not to make everything public. It is to **publish
the reusable pattern and never the deployment**.

## How to publish: a fresh snapshot, never the history

A private repository's history is where old secrets and personal data live, including files that were deleted
long ago. A public repository is **a new repository made from a scrubbed snapshot**, with a generic name. Issues
and pull requests do not come along either: they go public with every comment if you only flip the visibility.

The private repository stays the working copy. Or the public one becomes the upstream, and the district's own
deployment turns into configuration.

## The checklist

Every item needs evidence, recorded where the approver will read it. An item that does not apply, such as a
build for a repository of documents, needs a recorded reason instead.

1. **A generic name and description.** No district prefix and no hostnames.
2. **A secret scan over the full history** of the new repository: zero findings. Run the scanner against a planted
   secret as well, to prove it fires.
3. **A forbidden-string scan** is clean. Keep your own list, privately, of:
   - internal hostnames and server names;
   - private network addresses;
   - service account names;
   - database and linked-server names;
   - connection strings and anything shaped like a password assignment;
   - student names, and staff names other than credited builders.

   The list itself is a map of your estate. It stays out of every public repository.
4. **No data files.** No CSV, spreadsheet or saved email in git. Fixtures are synthetic and generated by a script.
5. **Configuration by example.** Ship an example settings file. Every district value comes from configuration.
6. **The standard files:** `LICENSE`, a `README` with the purpose, a "first 10 minutes" section, the commands and
   the credit, a `SECURITY.md`, and a `CLAUDE.md` map with an `AGENTS.md` pointer. Private vulnerability
   reporting is a repository setting, not a file, and GitHub offers it on public repositories. Turn it on straight
   after the visibility change and before you announce the repository, then check it is on.
7. **CI is green on a GitHub-hosted runner** with no access to the district network. That is evidence that the
   build and test paths the workflow exercises do not depend on you. It says nothing about paths it does not run.
8. **A second-district test**, where there is something to configure. A synthetic second configuration builds
   and runs.
9. **A second reviewer agrees it is shareable.** We use a different AI agent from the one that made the cut, and
   then a person approves.

## Choosing candidates

Rank by value to other districts divided by the effort to scrub.

| Kind of candidate | Why others want it | Scrub effort |
|---|---|---|
| A template born for the purpose | Nothing to scrub | Low |
| A small safety kit, such as SQL script guards | Small and universal | Low |
| A scanner or checker | Every district has the same deadline | Low to medium |
| A hosting pattern with its scripts | Hard-won and rarely written down | Medium |
| Agent skills | Useful, but skills carry hostnames. Publish a generic subset | High |
| A whole application | The largest surface. Do it last | High |

## Things that look harmless and are not

- **An inventory.** A list of your repositories, rulesets, runners or database grants maps your attack surface even
  when it contains no secret.
- **A scorecard of your own gaps.** It is honest and useful internally. Published, it is a list of what is not yet
  protected.
- **Numeric identifiers** for your own teams, apps and rulesets. They are not secrets, and they are also nobody
  else's business. Replace them with placeholders. A vendor's global constant is different: `15368`, the id of
  the GitHub Actions app, is the same for everyone and is fine to publish.
- **A decision log.** It names people, repositories and incidents on nearly every row.
